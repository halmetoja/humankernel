# Human Kernel Developmental Attractor Competition Model

**Version:** 0.2  
**Date:** 2026-08-17  
**Document type:** Self-contained theoretical context for humans and AI models  
**Former working name:** Human Kernel Bifurcation Model  
**Epistemic status:** Working mechanistic synthesis and research hypothesis; not an established psychological theory, diagnostic framework, or clinical instrument

> **Mandatory caveat:** The Human Kernel developmental attractor competition model described here is a theoretical hypothesis. Some of its components resemble or draw on established research traditions, but the proposed common compression mechanism, its blame-routing variables, its competing developmental attractors, and its computational topology have not been empirically demonstrated as a unified model. Do not present them as scientific fact.

---

## 1. Purpose and scope

This document records a proposed developmental model in which a child facing an emotionally and representationally difficult contradiction can stabilize the system through one of two simplified compression routes:

1. **self-compression:** preserve the caregiver or other as globally good by compressing the self into a globally bad representation; or
2. **other-compression:** preserve the self as globally good by compressing the other into a globally bad representation.

The central proposal is not merely that some people internalize and others externalize. It is that these may, in some cases, be **alternative stabilizing solutions to the same unresolved self–other integration problem**. Which solution becomes recurrent is hypothesized to depend on the child’s parameters, the social environment’s distribution of responses, the routing of blame and responsibility, and reinforcement history.

The preferred technical description is **developmental attractor competition**: two low-resolution regulatory configurations compete, and their basins of attraction depend on multiple child, relational, structural, and historical parameters. The former name *bifurcation model* implied a more specific mathematical result than the current model warrants. In dynamical-systems terms, a bifurcation would require a demonstrated qualitative change in the number, type, or stability of attractors when a defined control parameter crosses a critical value. No such control parameter or critical value has yet been established here.

The term **bifurcation** is therefore reserved for a possible future formal result. It should not be used as the default name of the present theory. A simulation may later reveal a genuine bifurcation—for example, if a formally defined simultaneity margin or relational-response parameter crosses a critical threshold—but the current high-dimensional hypothesis is more accurately an attractor competition model.

### 1.1 Technical and readable terminology

The technical terms are:

- **self-compression route / attractor:** the self-representation loses complexity and becomes globally devalued;
- **other-compression route / attractor:** the other-representation loses complexity and becomes globally devalued.

The earlier terms **self-sacrifice** and **other-sacrifice** are retained only as readable aliases. They describe which side’s complexity and goodness are lost, not an intentional act. The system does not consciously decide to sacrifice a person or representation.

Likewise, the compression routes are not necessarily deliberate “strategies.” They are representational configurations that remain available after simultaneity fails. Behaviors such as pleasing, blaming, withdrawal, control, apology, or repair are downstream regulatory actions generated from those configurations.

The model is part of the broader **Human Kernel** framing: the mind is described as an adaptive, resource-limited system that builds representations of self, others, and reality; uses them to predict and regulate; and must gradually develop enough separation, updateability, and fault tolerance to remain coherent under contradiction.

This document is designed to be usable without access to the conversation from which it was developed.

- **[H] Core hypothesis:** a central, presently unvalidated claim of this model.

## 3. Canonical summary

### 3.1 The core developmental contradiction

A young child depends on a caregiver for regulation, safety, and attachment. Over time, the child encounters data that a simple early model cannot comfortably represent:

```text
The caregiver is needed and loved
AND
the caregiver frustrates, fails, limits, withdraws, or hurts.

The child loves the caregiver
AND
the child can feel rage, aggression, disappointment, or hatred toward the caregiver.
```

“Hurts” here includes ordinary frustration and caregiver fallibility; it does not necessarily imply abuse. Severe adversity may intensify the problem but is not required by the model.

Mature integration would allow the system to maintain these propositions simultaneously:

```text
You did something that hurt me,
and you are still a whole person.

I acted badly,
and I am still a whole person.

We are connected,
and we remain separate minds.
```

The working hypothesis is that when contradiction load exceeds current integration and regulation capacity, the system may reduce complexity by routing global badness predominantly into one side of the self–other relation.

### 3.2 The proposed attractor competition

```text
                 CAREGIVER = LOVE + FRUSTRATION/HURT
                 SELF = LOVE + ANGER/AGGRESSION
                                  │
                                  ▼
            contradiction cannot currently be integrated
                                  │
                 ATTRACTOR COMPETITION
                         ╱           ╲
                        ▼             ▼
              SELF-COMPRESSION    OTHER-COMPRESSION
              "I am bad"          "You are bad"
              preserve other      preserve self
              and attachment      and self-coherence
```

These are the model’s two simplified low-resolution attractors. **Integration is not a third compression attractor**, because attractor competition is entered when current representational and regulatory capacity is insufficient to sustain integration. Integration is the higher-resolution developmental alternative that becomes available when the contradiction can be simultaneously held, regulated, differentiated, and updated.

The broader decision structure is therefore:

```text
contradictory self–other information
                │
                ▼
is sufficient effective simultaneity capacity available?
          ╱                         ╲
        YES                          NO
         │                            │
         ▼                            ▼
   INTEGRATION              ATTRACTOR COMPETITION
                         ╱                         ╲
                        ▼                           ▼
              SELF-COMPRESSION              OTHER-COMPRESSION
```

“Capacity available” is state-dependent. A person may integrate the same contradiction under low load but enter low-resolution attractor competition under attachment threat, shame, exhaustion, or intense arousal.

### 3.3 The model in one sentence

> **[H] When a dependent child cannot currently maintain a loved and needed caregiver’s goodness simultaneously with that caregiver’s frustration, fallibility, or harmfulness, the system may stabilize by compressing either the self-representation or the other-representation into a globally bad state; structural costs and the socially available routes for carrying blame and restoring regulation help determine which configuration becomes an attractor.**

### 3.4 Nature and nurture in the model

```text
TEMPERAMENT / PARTLY HERITABLE PARAMETERS
                    +
CAREGIVER AND NETWORK RESPONSE DISTRIBUTION
                    +
BLAME / RESPONSIBILITY ROUTING
                    +
DEVELOPMENTAL AND REINFORCEMENT HISTORY
                    ↓
        PROBABILITY LANDSCAPE OF STRATEGIES
                    ↓
      INTEGRATION OR A STABILIZED ATTRACTOR
```

The model therefore does not claim either genetic determinism or environmental determinism. Heritable parameters may shape sensitivity, costs, and learning rates. The relational environment shapes which strategies are available, punished, rewarded, modeled, and repeatedly successful.

## 4. Foundational assumptions

### 4.1 Dependency precedes autonomy

**[E/S]** A young child begins life dependent on external regulation. The caregiver is not merely an optional social object; the caregiver is part of the child’s effective regulatory environment.

In Human Kernel language:

```text
early state:
external regulator state → child state

later autonomous state:
external signal → interpretation/runtime layer → child response
```

Psychological development is therefore partly a movement from direct state coupling toward **process isolation**: the other person’s state can matter without directly overwriting the self’s entire state.

### 4.2 The mind operates through representations

**[E/M]** The model assumes that the mind maintains internal representations or generative models of self, other people, relationships, and the world. The process-industry concept of a **digital twin** is used as an analogy:

```text
REALITY
   ↓ signals, outcomes, prediction errors
INTERNAL MODEL / "DIGITAL TWIN"
   ↓ simulation and prediction
ACTION
   ↓
NEW REALITY DATA
   ↺
```

A healthy internal model is not one that is permanently correct. It is one that remains corrigible: it can be updated when reality violates its predictions without the whole system losing continuity.

### 4.3 Representation has resolution

**[H/O]** *Representational resolution* means how much relevant variation an internal model can preserve and use.

Low resolution:

```text
person → good OR bad
```

Higher resolution:

```text
person → caring, tired, angry, mistaken, independent,
         historically continuous, context-sensitive,
         capable of harm and repair
```

The model does not assume low resolution everywhere. A person may have sophisticated technical or social knowledge while self–other representations under attachment threat remain compressed and rigid.

### 4.4 Simultaneity and integration have costs

**[S/H]** This model adopts the **Simultaneity Principle from SRF Paper 9**: integration requires differentiated representations to remain active at the same time. Simultaneity is a prerequisite for integration, not a synonym for integration or psychological wholeness.

```text
differentiation
    ↓
the representations can vary separately
    ↓
simultaneity
    ↓
they remain active at the same time
    ↓
integration
    ↓
their relationship can be represented without deleting either one
```

Holding contradictory representations active is treated as cognitively and affectively costly. A person may understand both propositions when calm but lose their simultaneity under attachment threat or high arousal. Compression is the resulting low-resolution state when one side can no longer remain active; it is not the deepest mechanism by itself.

### 4.5 Ontological continuity can be threatened

**[H/M]** The term *kernel* refers to a minimal, ongoing sense that “I remain myself and continue to exist as a person across changing states.” When shame, criticism, separation, or guilt are directly coupled to this kernel, a local error can feel global:

```text
I did something bad
        ↓
I am entirely bad
        ↓
my attachment, worth, or existence is in danger
```

The integrated alternative localizes error:

```text
I did something harmful
        ↓
I can acknowledge responsibility
        ↓
I can repair while remaining a whole person
```

This “ontological threat” formulation is a Human Kernel hypothesis, not a standard diagnostic criterion.

### 4.6 Position within SRF Papers 9–10 and DIH

**Model status within SRF:** this document is best positioned as an **SRF developmental application and extension**, not as an independent parallel theory and not as a broader framework containing SRF.

The dependency is explicit:

```text
SRF PAPER 9 — SIMULTANEITY PRINCIPLE
supplies the processing constraint:
conflicting differentiated representations must remain active together
for integration to occur
                    ↓
SRF PAPER 10 — STRUCTURAL CONSEQUENCES AXIOM
supplies the topological selection pressure:
the cost of losing or degrading a representation depends on the
downstream structure that depends on it
                    ↓
HUMAN KERNEL DEVELOPMENTAL ATTRACTOR MODEL
adds developmental interaction:
when simultaneity fails, which side is compressed is shaped by
structural cost, caregiver-response distributions, blame routing,
immediate regulation, and reinforcement history
```

Paper 9 explains **why compression pressure appears**: current effective simultaneity capacity is insufficient to retain both sides of the contradiction. Paper 10 helps explain **why the two compression routes are not equally costly**. If a “good caregiver” representation supports attachment, safety, and continued regulation, degrading it may have extensive downstream consequences. If a “good self” representation supports identity, agency, worth, and ontological continuity, degrading that may instead be more structurally costly. The attractor model adds the hypothesis that the system tends toward the available configuration with the lower total predicted cost, and that relational feedback changes that cost over time.

This relationship can be summarized as:

```text
Paper 9:  Can both representations remain active?
Paper 10: What else is endangered if either one degrades?
This model: Which low-resolution configuration is repeatedly selected,
            socially stabilized, and learned when both cannot remain active?
```

The model does **not** settle the **Developmental Irreversibility Hypothesis (DIH)**. Attractor stability, path dependence, and hysteresis are weaker claims than irreversibility. Three possibilities remain open:

1. **structural rewriting:** later experience can alter the original dependency topology;
2. **functional overlay:** new regulatory routes can suppress or bypass an older topology without replacing it;
3. **partial developmental irreversibility:** some early structural constraints persist even when behavior and conscious understanding improve.

The present model must allow these alternatives to compete. A simulation must not implement DIH as a fixed rule. It should separately measure behavioral adaptation, new overlay routes, changes in original edge structure, recovery under load, and reactivation of the earlier attractor under stress.

## 5. The compression attractors and the integration alternative

### 5.1 Integration: a mechanistic account

Integration preserves the complexity and continuity of both self and other, but it is not a cost-free default. It is an available operation only when effective simultaneity capacity is sufficient for the current contradiction load.

Define the provisional **simultaneity margin**:

```text
M_t = effective_simultaneity_capacity_t − contradiction_load_t

effective_simultaneity_capacity_t =
    developmental_capacity
  + caregiver_scaffolding
  + learned_regulation_and_buffering
  - arousal_penalty
  - fatigue_and_context_load
```

When `M_t` is sufficiently positive, integration can be attempted. When it is strongly negative, both contradictory sides cannot remain active and the two compression configurations compete. This is a probabilistic capacity gate, not yet a validated psychological equation.

The proposed **integration operator** has seven steps:

```text
1. CO-ACTIVATE
   Keep the positive and negative self/other representations active together.
        ↓
2. DIFFERENTIATE SOURCE AND STATE OWNERSHIP
   "Your anger is your state; my fear is my state; the relationship is another state."
        ↓
3. LOCALIZE THE ERROR
   Distinguish event, action, temporary state, stable trait, and whole identity.
        ↓
4. ESTIMATE RESPONSIBILITY
   Allocate causal and moral responsibility across self, other, context,
   uncertainty, and chance without requiring one globally bad carrier.
        ↓
5. SELECT A RESPONSE
   Seek information, set a boundary, apologize, repair, tolerate uncertainty,
   or disengage safely as the evidence requires.
        ↓
6. OBSERVE THE OUTCOME
   Test whether connection, safety, prediction, and agency can survive conflict.
        ↓
7. UPDATE WITHOUT DELETION
   Revise the relevant representations and edge weights while preserving
   the continuity and complexity of both persons.
```

The output is therefore not a compromise valence such as “both are partly good.” It is a **structured, higher-resolution relation** in which different propositions can remain true, be weighted by evidence, and change independently.

#### Immediate costs of integration

- sustained arousal while both sides remain active;
- working-memory and attentional demand;
- delayed relief compared with rapid global blame;
- uncertainty while responsibility and meaning are estimated;
- risk that protest, guilt, or boundary-setting may threaten connection.

#### Immediate and near-term rewards of integration

- the caregiver remains available despite disagreement or wrongdoing;
- arousal decreases **without deleting either representation**;
- uncertainty falls because the event receives a more accurate local explanation;
- a boundary, apology, or repair changes the interaction effectively;
- the child experiences agency without global control or global submission;
- future prediction improves and similar ruptures become less catastrophic;
- the self learns: “I can be responsible without disappearing”; and
- the relationship learns: “conflict can end in repair rather than world-switching.”

Early in development these rewards may be too delayed to compete with the rapid relief of compression. Caregiver co-regulation is therefore not merely comforting; it acts as **computational scaffolding** that pays part of integration’s immediate affective cost until the child can increasingly perform the sequence internally.

Guilt can be adaptive in this route when it means “I harmed someone I value and can respond to that,” rather than “I am globally bad and should disappear.” The distinction between **guilt about an act**, **global shame about the self**, and **annihilating self-condemnation** is essential.

Integration does not mean passivity, automatic forgiveness, denial of abuse, or equal responsibility in unequal situations. It permits the conclusion that one person is substantially or entirely responsible for an event while still avoiding a totalizing theory of that person or the self.

### 5.2 Self-compression route

**Core compression:** preserve the caregiver or relationship as globally good or necessary by assigning global badness to the self.

```text
caregiver frustrates, harms, or withdraws
        ↓
"the caregiver is unsafe/bad" predicts attachment danger
        ↓
"I am the problem" preserves a good/necessary caregiver
        ↓
self-monitoring, submission, pleasing, need suppression
        ↓
conflict or abandonment threat decreases
        ↓
strategy is reinforced
```

Possible functional advantages in the original environment:

- preserves attachment to a needed regulator;
- creates an illusion or partial reality of control: “If I change myself, safety may return”;
- makes the caregiver’s behavior more predictable;
- may reduce retaliation when anger toward the caregiver is prohibited;
- may obtain care, approval, or reduced conflict through compliance.

Possible later correlates, none of which are necessary or sufficient:

- chronic self-blame or global shame;
- hypervigilance to others’ moods;
- pleasing, submission, invisibility, or compulsive responsibility;
- inhibition of anger and needs;
- internalizing symptoms;
- difficulty locating responsibility accurately;
- vulnerability to exploitative relationships.

The readable alias *self-sacrifice* refers to the loss of self-representational complexity in this route. It does not imply intention, moral nobility, conscious consent, or a fixed personality type. Technical analysis should prefer *self-compression*.

### 5.3 Other-compression route

**Core compression:** preserve the self as globally good, special, coherent, or blameless by assigning global badness to the other.

```text
frustration, shame, failure, or conflict
        ↓
self-implication predicts identity/ontological danger
        ↓
"you are the problem"
        ↓
devaluation, blame, control, entitlement, or projection
        ↓
other submits, apologizes, admires, or restores regulation
        ↓
distress and shame decrease
        ↓
strategy is reinforced
```

Possible functional advantages in the original environment:

- rapidly reduces shame or self-disorganization;
- protects a valued or grandiose self-model;
- may successfully recruit others as external regulators;
- can obtain submission, exceptions, admiration, or control;
- may preserve agency where vulnerability is experienced as dangerous.

Possible later correlates, none of which are necessary or sufficient:

- externalization of responsibility;
- devaluation and binary judgments;
- control of close others as regulators;
- shame sensitivity hidden by attack or superiority;
- entitlement or admiration-seeking;
- externalizing behavior;
- some narcissistic defenses or traits.

This route is **not equivalent to narcissistic personality disorder**, and the model must not use “narcissist” as a casual synonym for any person who externalizes blame. Externalization can be transient, contextual, developmentally ordinary, or associated with many different conditions.

The readable alias *other-sacrifice* refers to the loss of the other’s representational complexity. It does not imply a conscious decision to sacrifice another person. Technical analysis should prefer *other-compression*.

### 5.4 Mixed, alternating, and context-dependent solutions

The model does not require a person to remain on one route. Possible patterns include:

- self-compression with powerful people and other-compression with less powerful people;
- self-blame at baseline and externalized rage under overload;
- grandiose self-protection followed by collapse into global shame;
- different routes in different relationships;
- repeated switching between “good world” and “bad world” states.

**[H]** The most realistic model is therefore dimensional and state-dependent. “Route” means a recurrent probability distribution, not a permanent binary type.

### 5.5 Comparison table

| Dimension | Self-compression | Other-compression | Integration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global problem location | Self | Other | Specific acts, states, and conditions |
| Immediate protected object | Caregiver/attachment | Self-coherence or self-value | Continuity of both self and other |
| Typical fast regulation | Submit, please, inhibit | Blame, devalue, control | Reflect, regulate, differentiate, repair |
| Control logic | “If I fix myself, connection may return” | “If the other yields, my stability returns” | “We can update without global collapse” |
| Common cost | Shame, need loss, hypervigilance | Reality distortion, conflict, dependency on external regulation | Greater short-term affective and cognitive load |
| Responsibility | Over-assumed | Under-assumed or displaced | Proportionately allocated |
| Model update | Self is globally rewritten | Other/reality is globally rewritten | Localized evidence updates a complex model |

## 6. Caregiver response distribution

### 6.1 The environment is a distribution, not a single event

**[H]** The model proposes that development depends less on one caregiver reaction than on the **distribution of reactions across repeated interactions and across the child’s relational network**.

The relevant environment may include:

- parents and other primary caregivers;
- siblings and extended family;
- teachers, peers, and institutions;
- cultural rules about obedience, anger, guilt, status, and repair;
- the child’s changing position and power in the system.

Therefore, “hypervigilant mother” should be treated only as one illustrative scenario, not as a privileged cause. The theory is not a mother-blaming model. Caregiver gender is not a core variable.

### 6.2 Response classes

When a child expresses distress, anger, accusation, withdrawal, or self-blame, the surrounding system may respond through different mixtures of:

- attunement and co-regulation;
- accepting legitimate responsibility;
- accepting global or false blame;
- shame, humiliation, retaliation, or counterattack;
- withdrawal or attachment threat;
- capitulation and removal of boundaries;
- overvaluation or exceptional treatment;
- consistent limits without humiliation;
- mentalization and curiosity;
- repair after rupture;
- differentiated redistribution of responsibility;
- unpredictability or chaotic alternation among these responses.

### 6.3 Blame and responsibility routing

The question “Who can carry the blame?” is a central proposed control parameter.

```text
A. Child carries global blame
   → self-compression can stabilize

B. Caregiver accepts global blame and restores regulation
   → other-compression can stabilize

C. Nobody can tolerate blame
   → escalation, chaos, rapid switching, or rigid splitting may stabilize

D. Responsibility is differentiated
   → integration becomes learnable
```

The model distinguishes **blame** from **responsibility**:

- *Blame* often functions here as globalized badness: someone must become “the bad node.”
- *Responsibility* can be local, proportional, behavioral, and repair-oriented.

An integrated response can say:

> “I was wrong to shout at you, and you are still responsible for hitting. We can both remain connected while each action is named accurately.”

This avoids both caregiver collapse and retaliatory shaming. It teaches that responsibility can be divided without dividing the world into an entirely good party and an entirely bad party.

### 6.4 Reinforcing other-compression

```text
child externalizes blame
        ↓
caregiver accepts the global bad role, submits, or over-apologizes
        ↓
caregiver restores the child's regulation
        ↓
distress falls
        ↓
externalization becomes more probable next time
```

**[H]** Externalizing regulation should become more stable when the relational environment repeatedly contains agents who accept global blame, surrender boundaries, or provide soothing contingent on the child’s accusation or dominance.

This does not mean that listening, apologizing, or taking real responsibility is harmful. The proposed risk is **contingent regulation through undifferentiated surrender**, not humane responsiveness.

### 6.5 Reinforcing self-compression

```text
child blames or protests against caregiver
        ↓
caregiver retaliates, shames, withdraws, or threatens attachment
        ↓
externalization becomes dangerous
        ↓
child redirects blame toward self and suppresses protest
        ↓
connection or calm returns
        ↓
self-compression becomes more probable next time
```

Self-compression can also be reinforced when compliance receives warmth while autonomous disagreement reliably loses it.

### 6.6 Clarifying “blame absorption capacity”

An earlier working term was **Blame Absorption Capacity (BAC)**. It risks conflating three different phenomena and should be decomposed for research:

1. **Affective Containment Capacity (ACC) [O]:** ability to tolerate another’s anger, accusation, or distress without retaliating, collapsing, or abandoning the relationship.
2. **Undifferentiated Blame Assumption (UBA) [O]:** tendency to accept global or inaccurate blame and give up one’s boundaries in order to restore calm.
3. **Responsibility Redistribution Capacity (RRC) [O]:** ability to acknowledge one’s real share, reject false global badness, return the other’s share without humiliation, and support repair.

Healthy caregiving is hypothesized to combine **high ACC**, **low UBA**, and **high RRC**. High UBA without RRC may reinforce other-compression. Low ACC combined with retaliation or withdrawal may reinforce self-compression.

These are proposed constructs, not validated scales.

### 6.7 The “blame market” metaphor

**[M]** A family may be imagined as a market or network in which contradiction, shame, and blame are distributed. Some members repeatedly become absorbers, scapegoats, judges, rescuers, or untouchable nodes. The metaphor highlights systemic allocation but must not imply that blame is a literal conserved substance.

## 7. Temperament, genetics, and gene–environment dynamics

### 7.1 What may be partly heritable

**[E/S]** Personality-related traits and vulnerabilities can show genetic influence. Within this model, what is inherited is not assumed to be a completed “narcissistic topology.” More plausible inherited or biologically influenced parameters include:

- affective reactivity;
- negative emotionality;
- threat sensitivity;
- reward and status sensitivity;
- shame sensitivity;
- dominance or approach tendencies;
- impulsivity;
- inhibitory control;
- aggression proneness;
- social sensitivity;
- baseline soothing and recovery rates;
- differential susceptibility to environmental input.

### 7.2 Parameters alter costs, not destiny

**[H]** Temperament changes the cost landscape. For example:

- high threat reactivity may make attachment rupture especially costly;
- high shame sensitivity may make self-implication especially costly;
- high dominance and low inhibition may make outward control easier;
- high social sensitivity may increase learning from caregiver responses;
- slow recovery may strengthen whichever strategy produces immediate relief.

The same environment can therefore affect children differently, and the same child parameters can develop differently in different response distributions.

### 7.3 Bidirectional and evocative effects

The child is not a passive recipient. A child’s behavior changes caregiver behavior, which changes the child’s learning environment:

```text
child parameter → child behavior → caregiver response
       ↑                                  ↓
       └──────────── new learning ────────┘
```

This permits gene–environment correlation, feedback, sibling divergence, and developmental path dependence without assuming that any participant consciously designs the outcome.

### 7.4 What the model does not claim

- Heritability does not mean immutability.
- Environmental influence does not mean parental blame.
- A genetic association with narcissistic traits would not prove this attractor-competition mechanism.
- A caregiver response correlated with a route may be a response to the child, a cause, a mediator, a reciprocal effect, or a product of a third factor.
- Neither route is biologically or socially inevitable.

### 7.5 Developmental timing and consolidation

The current model does **not** posit one universal age at which an attractor is selected. It distinguishes three questions:

1. **When does low simultaneity occur?** From infancy onward, and later whenever load temporarily exceeds capacity.
2. **When can integration be scaffolded and learned?** Gradually, as differentiation, memory, language, regulation, mentalization, and social understanding develop.
3. **When does a repeated solution become a stable cross-context topology?** Unknown; this is a central empirical question.

A provisional developmental map is:

| Period | Expected relevance to the model | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Infancy, approximately 0–2 years | External regulation is high, self–other models are early, and low-resolution state organization is developmentally ordinary. Compression here must not itself be pathologized. Repeated rupture–response–repair distributions may establish early priors. | [S/H] |
| Early childhood, approximately 2–5 years | Language, autobiographical organization, self–other differentiation, inhibitory control, and explicit understanding of minds expand. Caregivers increasingly scaffold responsibility, mixed feelings, protest, and repair. Route-specific reinforcement may become more behaviorally visible. | [S/H] |
| Middle childhood | School, peers, rules, performance, shame, and wider authority networks test whether mixed representations and responsibility remain available outside the primary dyad. Strategies may generalize or be revised. | [H] |
| Adolescence | Identity, status, sexuality, autonomy, and peer attachment reorganize structural centrality. Earlier solutions may consolidate, destabilize, or be recruited into a new topology. | [H/X] |
| Adulthood | The same simultaneity gate remains state-dependent. Attachment threat, humiliation, fatigue, loss, or power differences may reactivate compression; stable relationships, reflection, and treatment may support new learning. Adult activation does not identify the age of origin. | [S/H] |

The key Paper 9 variable is therefore not chronological age alone but:

```text
effective simultaneity capacity(age, current state, relationship, load)
```

Development should generally expand capacity and internal scaffolding, while acute load can temporarily reduce them. Early compression is expected and adaptive; theoretical concern begins when a particular low-resolution solution becomes **rigid, generalized, structurally central, difficult to reverse, or unable to recover after load falls**.

The most cautious current hypothesis is a **developmentally sensitive but lifespan-active model**. Infancy and early childhood may be especially important for priors and topology, but the present theory does not justify a fixed critical period. DIH remains open because consolidation, later overlay, and true structural rewriting have not yet been distinguished.

## 8. Reinforcement loops and attractor formation

### 8.1 No single magical decision point

The “solution moment” should not normally be interpreted as one conscious or irreversible childhood choice. A more plausible formulation is a large number of micro-episodes:

```text
contradiction or rupture
        ↓
candidate representational configuration and regulatory behavior
        ↓
caregiver/network response
        ↓
immediate affective and attachment outcome
        ↓
learning update
        ↓
new priors for the next episode
```

Early episodes alter later starting conditions. Repetition makes one strategy easier to retrieve, more expected to work, and more tightly coupled to the self–other model.

### 8.2 Self-compression reinforcement loop

```text
monitor / please / accept blame
        ↓
caregiver becomes calmer or remains connected
        ↓
attachment threat falls
        ↓
"self-reduction produces safety" is strengthened
        ↺
```

### 8.3 Other-compression reinforcement loop

```text
externalize / devalue / dominate
        ↓
other apologizes, submits, admires, or regulates
        ↓
shame and disorganization fall
        ↓
"other-reduction protects the self" is strengthened
        ↺
```

### 8.4 Integration reinforcement loop

```text
contradiction appears while the simultaneity gate remains open
        ↓
self and other representations remain co-active
        ↓
caregiver contains arousal, keeps connection, and maintains boundaries
        ↓
the event and responsibility are localized accurately
        ↓
boundary, apology, information-seeking, or repair changes the outcome
        ↓
distress falls without global self- or other-devaluation
        ↓
prediction becomes more accurate and connection/agency survives
        ↓
the value and future availability of integration increase
        ↺
```

The concrete reinforcing event is not abstract maturity. It is the experienced contingency:

```text
"I kept both realities active, acted on the specific problem,
and the world became safer or more predictable without either person disappearing."
```

The integration reward can be represented as a weighted combination of preserved connection, successful repair, improved prediction, effective agency, and distress reduction without representational deletion. Its initial delay and processing cost explain why caregiver scaffolding is especially important early in development.

### 8.5 Attractors and hysteresis

**[H/X]** Once a strategy is embedded, it may become an attractor: many different triggers lead the system toward the same regulatory configuration. Path dependence may produce hysteresis, meaning that changing the current environment is not immediately enough to reverse the learned topology. Reversal may require repeated corrective experiences, new regulation skills, safer relationships, or deliberate therapy.

Attractor language is useful only if it eventually yields measurable state transitions, basins of attraction, and parameter-dependent changes. Until then it remains partly metaphorical.

Hysteresis must not be treated as evidence for DIH. A system can resist change yet remain structurally rewritable. Conversely, behavioral improvement may reflect a compensatory overlay while the earlier attractor remains easy to reactivate under load. These possibilities require different state and topology measures.

### 8.6 Short-term adaptation versus long-term cost

Both compression routes may be locally rational in their original environment. Their immediate reward can coexist with later costs. The model therefore avoids calling childhood strategies irrational defects. It asks:

> Which available response minimized predicted danger for this system, at this developmental stage, in this relational environment?

## 9. Human Kernel computational framing

### 9.1 Core architecture

**[M/H]** The Human Kernel analogy describes the following layers:

```text
BIOLOGICAL PARAMETERS / HARDWARE-LIKE CONSTRAINTS
                         ↓
KERNEL: continuity of self, basic agency, survival relevance
                         ↓
PSYCHOLOGICAL RUNTIME / MEDIATION LAYER
  interpretation, mentalization, affect regulation, alternatives,
  provenance, confidence, error localization, boundary enforcement
                         ↓
REPRESENTATIONAL NETWORK / DIGITAL TWIN
  models of self, others, relationships, actions, and the world
                         ↓
PREDICTION, ACTION, FEEDBACK, UPDATE
```

This is not a literal neuroanatomical map. It is a constraint-generating analogy.

### 9.2 Process isolation

Early or poorly isolated state coupling:

```text
caregiver state changes
        ↓
child's whole self-state changes
```

More developed isolation:

```text
caregiver state changes
        ↓
child represents the caregiver's state
        ↓
child distinguishes it from self-state
        ↓
child updates locally while preserving continuity
```

Process isolation does not mean emotional detachment. It means: **your state can affect me without becoming my entire state or defining my entire self.**

### 9.3 Buffer and integration nodes

**[X/O]** A smallest useful developmental increment may be a mediating representation between an event and the kernel:

```text
"Mother criticized me"
        ↓
"She may be tired, mistaken, or responding to one action"
        ↓
"Our relationship contains more than this event"
        ↓
"I remain myself"
```

Possible computational names include *buffer node*, *reflective node*, *integration node*, or *transitional representation*. The point is not positive thinking. The buffer holds multiple hypotheses long enough to prevent a raw event from globally rewriting identity.

### 9.4 Update versus protect

An integrated digital twin uses prediction error to improve its model:

```text
data conflicts with model
        ↓
evaluate reliability and context
        ↓
update model proportionately
```

A defensive route may instead preserve an old core model:

```text
data conflicts with model
        ↓
update threatens attachment or self-continuity
        ↓
compress, reject, reinterpret, or relocate the data
        ↓
preserve old model
```

Self-compression and other-compression differ in **which part of the model is degraded and which structurally central part is protected**.

### 9.5 Topological framing

Representations can be modeled as nodes and dependencies as edges. A rigid system may contain highly central “good self,” “bad self,” “good other,” or “bad other” hubs. When a hub switches, many dependent interpretations switch with it:

```text
GOOD WORLD                   BAD WORLD
good self                    bad or endangered self
good other                   bad/persecuting other
safe future                  collapsed future
connection                   abandonment/threat
```

**[H/X]** Splitting may therefore involve not only a binary judgment about one person but a switch between poorly integrated world-model networks. Following Paper 10’s Structural Consequences Axiom, the pressure to protect a node depends partly on what else would be destabilized by its degradation. The developmental attractor model adds that, after simultaneity fails, the system can reduce contradiction by protecting the more structurally costly side and compressing the other side into global badness.

### 9.6 Resource management

The system has finite affective, attentional, and regulatory resources. A simulation should distinguish:

- **effective simultaneity capacity:** state-dependent ability to keep differentiated conflicting representations jointly active, as required by Paper 9;
- **contradiction load:** current demand created by mismatch and affect;
- **compression threshold:** load at which lower-resolution strategies become more probable;
- **recovery rate:** speed at which capacity and resolution return after activation;
- **updateability:** readiness of a representation to change with evidence;
- **centrality:** how many other predictions depend on a representation;
- **structural consequence cost:** predicted downstream disruption if a node loses resolution or changes state.

These are working abstractions, not validated psychological quantities.

## 10. Minimal formal model

The following formalization is illustrative. It is intended to make the hypothesis testable, not to claim that the mind literally computes these equations.

### 10.1 Episode state

At interaction episode `t`, define:

- `X_t`: child state, including affect, attachment security, self/other valence, current load, and learned configuration values;
- `E_t`: relational environment state and caregiver response distribution;
- `Z_t`: event producing contradictory or threatening information;
- `M_t`: effective simultaneity margin from Section 5.1;
- `G_t`: probabilistic integration-availability gate derived from `M_t`;
- `K_t`: resulting representational configuration—`I` (integration), `SC` (self-compression), or `OC` (other-compression);
- `B_t`: regulatory behavior generated from the current configuration and context;
- `R_t`: caregiver/network response;
- `Y_t`: immediate outcome, such as distress reduction, attachment change, shame change, or repair.

The available configuration set depends on the gate:

```text
if G_t is open:    K_t ∈ {I, SC, OC}
if G_t is closed:  K_t ∈ {SC, OC}
```

An implementation may also include an unorganized overload state, but integration must not remain selectable by definition after the model has declared simultaneity impossible.

### 10.2 Strategy cost

For each candidate configuration `k`, an illustrative expected cost is:

```text
C_t(k) =
    w_attach  × predicted_attachment_threat(k)
  + w_self    × predicted_self_continuity_threat(k)
  + w_affect  × unregulated_affect(k)
  + w_effort  × integration_effort(k)
  + w_social  × expected_social_penalty(k)
  + w_struct  × structural_consequence_cost(k)
  - w_relief  × expected_immediate_relief(k)
  - w_repair  × expected_repair_value(k)
  - w_predict × expected_prediction_improvement(k)
  - w_agency  × expected_effective_agency(k)
```

`structural_consequence_cost(k)` implements the Paper 10 link. It estimates the weighted downstream disturbance caused by compressing or changing a representation, not merely the representation’s subjective importance.

The weights vary by temperament, development, current load, and history. Strategy probability could be modeled with a softmax or another stochastic choice rule:

```text
P(K_t = k) ∝ exp(-β × C_t(k))
```

This expresses probabilistic, not conscious, selection.

### 10.3 Environment response

The caregiver response is sampled conditionally:

```text
R_t ~ P(response | B_t, K_t, caregiver state,
        relationship history, network role, context)
```

It is important that the response distribution is dynamic. Caregiver fatigue, stress, learning, the child’s behavior, and other relationships can all change it.

### 10.4 Learning update

An elementary reinforcement update could be:

```text
Q_{t+1}(K_t) = Q_t(K_t)
                 + α × [r_t + γ max_k Q_t(k) - Q_t(K_t)]
```

where the learning signal reflects immediate regulation, attachment preservation, social consequences, delayed costs, and repair. A realistic model should allow immediate rewards to dominate early learning while delayed interpersonal costs accumulate later.

For integration, the immediate reward must be computed explicitly rather than assigned as “maturity”:

```text
r_I =
    w_connection × connection_preserved
  + w_repair     × repair_success
  + w_accuracy   × prediction_error_reduction
  + w_agency     × effective_local_action
  + w_relief     × distress_reduction_without_deletion
  - w_effort     × simultaneity_and_processing_cost
  - w_delay      × delay_to_relief
```

For `SC` and `OC`, immediate relief can be faster, while later relational, predictive, and structural costs may be larger. This timing difference is part of the hypothesis and must not be hidden by using only one-step rewards.

### 10.5 Representational update

The configuration, generated behavior, and caregiver response also change the network:

```text
node valence
node confidence
node updateability
self–other differentiation
co-activation tolerance
edge strengths
hub centrality
strategy retrieval threshold
```

Repeated local updates may produce a global topology. The topology must emerge from rules; the simulation must not contain code equivalent to “if child externalizes, create narcissistic topology.”

### 10.6 Formal criteria for attractor and bifurcation claims

An **attractor** claim would require more than stable-looking categories. At minimum, a formal model should show that:

- trajectories from multiple nearby initial states converge toward a common state region;
- the state region persists under bounded perturbation;
- basins of attraction can be estimated;
- transitions and recovery can be distinguished from observational noise; and
- the result is robust across plausible parameter ranges and not created by labels in the code.

A **bifurcation** claim would additionally require:

1. a defined control parameter `λ`;
2. a critical value or region `λ_c`;
3. a qualitative change in equilibrium, attractor number, attractor stability, or oscillatory behavior across `λ_c`; and
4. continuation, sensitivity, and alternative-model analyses showing that the change is not merely a smooth probability shift.

Candidate parameters such as simultaneity margin, structural-cost asymmetry, or caregiver RRC may be investigated, but none is currently established as *the* control parameter. Until these criteria are met, the correct claim is multidimensional attractor competition.

## 11. Simulation specification

### 11.1 Research goal

Test whether self-compression and other-compression attractors can emerge from shared local rules under different parameter and caregiver-response distributions, and whether a stable integrated trajectory emerges under sufficient simultaneity, reliable containment, boundaries, differentiated responsibility, and repair.

The simulation should be able to disconfirm the model. It may produce:

```text
self-compression dominates
other-compression dominates
integration dominates
mixed switching
no stable attractor
parameter-dependent or null result
```

### 11.2 Agents

Minimum viable model:

- one child agent;
- one or more caregiver agents;
- optional peer/sibling/network agents;
- event generator;
- representational network and learning engine.

### 11.3 Child parameters

All ranges below are illustrative normalized values, not clinical scores.

| `social_susceptibility` | Sensitivity to relational feedback | 0–1 |

### 11.6 Dynamic state variables

- current distress and arousal;
- current attachment security;
- current self and other valence;
- contradiction load;
- representational resolution;
- capacity for simultaneous activation;
- perceived controllability;
- ontological/self-continuity threat;
- effective simultaneity margin and integration-gate state;
- learned configuration values for integration, self-compression, and other-compression;
- confidence and provenance of each representation;
- history of rupture and repair;
- dependence on external regulation;
- caregiver state and available capacity.

### 11.7 Event loop

```text
1. Sample a relational event and its ambiguity/severity.
2. Generate prediction error and contradiction load.
3. Compute effective simultaneity margin from developmental capacity,
   caregiver scaffolding, learned regulation, arousal, and other load.
4. Open or close the probabilistic integration gate.
5. Sample a representational configuration from the currently available set.
6. Generate regulatory behavior from configuration, state, and context.
7. Sample caregiver/network responses conditionally.
8. Calculate immediate relief, shame, attachment, prediction, agency,
   structural, and social outcomes.
9. Update configuration values and expectations.
10. Update self–other representations and graph topology locally.
11. Distinguish structural rewriting from compensatory overlay learning.
12. Apply delayed consequences, repair, recovery, and stress reactivation.
13. Advance developmental time, record state transitions, and repeat.
```

### 11.8 Required output metrics

- frequency and persistence of each strategy;
- integration rate;
- self-blame and other-blame distributions;
- accuracy of responsibility allocation;
- representational resolution;
- co-activation tolerance for contradictory features;
- frequency of global world switches;
- shame and attachment volatility;
- repair success rate;
- dependency on external regulation;
- topology centrality and fragility;
- original-edge change versus added overlay routes;
- low-load competence versus high-load attractor reactivation;
- time and intervention dose needed to leave an attractor;
- within-person context switching;
- between-run variability under identical starting parameters.

### 11.9 Experimental design

Useful experiments include:

1. **Matched-child counterfactuals:** hold child parameters fixed and vary caregiver distributions.
2. **Matched-environment counterfactuals:** hold environment fixed and vary temperament parameters.
3. **Factorial interactions:** test whether temperament and response patterns interact rather than merely add.
4. **Network substitution:** add one caregiver with high ACC and RRC to an otherwise rigid system.
5. **Ablation:** remove blame routing, repair, or process isolation to test whether each adds explanatory power.
6. **Reversal:** change the environment after an attractor forms to estimate hysteresis, recovery, structural rewriting, and overlay learning separately.
7. **DIH model comparison:** compare rewritable-topology, functional-overlay, and partial-irreversibility variants against the same trajectories.
8. **Alternative models:** compare attractor competition with simpler separate internalizing and externalizing models.
9. **Out-of-sample prediction:** estimate parameters on early episodes and predict later behavior.

### 11.10 Anti-circularity requirements

The simulation must not:

- assign the route directly from a diagnostic label;
- make self-compression or other-compression inevitable by definition;
- encode DIH as true rather than comparing it with rewriting and overlay alternatives;
- hard-code that one topology always collapses;
- count programmed route names as emergent evidence;
- treat a visual resemblance as validation;
- tune and evaluate on the same trajectories without reporting it.

The strongest computational result would be the spontaneous emergence of recognizable strategies and phase changes from psychologically interpretable local assumptions.

## 12. Falsifiable predictions

These predictions are provisional and require operational definitions before empirical testing.

### Prediction 1: Contingent global-blame absorption

**[H]** After baseline child behavior and relevant confounds are controlled, repeated sequences in which externalized blame is followed by caregiver capitulation and successful soothing should predict an increased probability of later externalization in similar contexts.

**Challenge to the model:** no prospective association, no within-person learning effect, or a relationship fully explained by stable prior externalizing behavior without reciprocal reinforcement.

### Prediction 2: Attachment punishment and self-blame

**[H]** When protest or blame directed toward a caregiver reliably produces withdrawal, retaliation, or humiliation, while self-blame/compliance restores connection, later self-blame and protest inhibition should increase.

**Challenge:** the response contingency does not predict change after controlling for prior self-blame, temperament, and adversity.

### Prediction 3: Differentiated responsibility favors integration

**[H]** High ACC, low UBA, high RRC, consistent boundaries, and reliable repair should predict greater ability to acknowledge personal wrongdoing without global self-condemnation or global other-condemnation.

**Challenge:** differentiated responsibility and repair show no relation to integration measures or perform no better than warmth alone.

### Prediction 4: Immediate relief mediates reinforcement

**[H]** The effect of a compression route on its future use should be partly mediated by immediate reduction in distress, shame, or attachment threat.

**Challenge:** strategy repetition is unrelated to immediate regulatory consequences.

### Prediction 5: Shared latent integration limitation

**[H]** Predominantly self-compressing and other-compressing groups should both show difficulty maintaining mixed self–other representations under attachment load, despite opposite attribution directions.

**Challenge:** one route shows no relevant integration limitation, or separate mechanisms explain the groups substantially better without a common latent factor.

### Prediction 6: Temperament moderates, but does not dictate, route

**[H]** Shame sensitivity, threat reactivity, dominance, inhibition, and social susceptibility should interact with caregiver response distributions. No single parameter should deterministically select a route.

**Challenge:** route outcomes are almost entirely fixed by either temperament or environment with no meaningful interaction or path dependence.

### Prediction 7: Sibling divergence is structured

**[H]** Siblings in the same household may diverge because they evoke and receive different response distributions, occupy different system roles, and have different temperamental costs. Measured child-specific contingencies should explain more variance than household-level averages alone.

**Challenge:** child-specific relational distributions add no predictive value.

### Prediction 8: State-dependent route switching

**[H]** Some people should switch from self-compression to other-compression as load, power, or relationship context changes. A dimensional state model should outperform rigid categorical typing.

**Challenge:** route expression is fully context-invariant or categories explain substantially better.

### Prediction 9: Hysteresis after environmental change

**[X]** Once strongly learned, a route should persist temporarily after the original contingency changes, then weaken through repeated non-confirming and reparative experiences.

**Challenge:** behavior tracks current contingencies immediately with no path dependence, or remains entirely fixed despite extensive corrective learning.

### Prediction 10: Model-specific incremental validity

**[H]** Measures of blame routing, response contingencies, contradiction co-activation, and responsibility redistribution should improve prediction beyond established measures of attachment, parenting style, temperament, internalizing, and externalizing.

**Decisive challenge:** the proposed variables add no reliable explanatory or predictive value. In that case the developmental attractor model may be new terminology for existing constructs rather than a distinct theory.

### Prediction 11: Emergence in simulation

**[X]** Under some parameter regions, the same local learning rules should produce distinct self-compression, other-compression, integrated, and unstable trajectories without route-specific outcome rules.

**Challenge:** distinct attractors arise only when effectively encoded in advance, or the proposed local rules do not produce them under plausible parameter ranges.

### Prediction 12: Corrective response profile matters

**[X]** Pure reassurance or pure confrontation should be less effective at shifting an other-compression attractor than containment plus boundaries plus differentiated responsibility and repair. For a self-compression attractor, simple praise should be less effective than safe protest, accurate responsibility allocation, and maintained connection.

**Challenge:** response profile does not matter once nonspecific warmth or attention is controlled.

### Prediction 13: Integration has identifiable reinforcing contingencies

**[H]** After successful co-activation of conflicting representations, preservation of connection, successful local action or repair, improved prediction, and distress reduction without global devaluation should each increase the probability of later integration. Caregiver scaffolding should be especially predictive when the child’s unassisted simultaneity capacity is low.

**Challenge:** integration learning is unrelated to these outcomes, or it can be explained entirely by general cognitive maturation without relational contingency effects.

### Prediction 14: Structural consequences affect compression direction

**[H]** The relative downstream dependency cost of degrading the “good self” versus “good caregiver” representation should predict self- versus other-compression beyond current emotional intensity and belief content alone.

**Challenge:** topology-derived structural consequence measures add no predictive value beyond affect, attachment strength, shame, and ordinary attribution measures.

### Prediction 15: Occurrence and consolidation have different age profiles

**[H]** Low-resolution compression should be common and relatively fluid early in development. With increasing effective simultaneity capacity, ordinary development should show improved recovery and mixed-representation maintenance. Maladaptive significance should be better predicted by rigidity, generalization, structural centrality, and failure to recover than by early compression episodes themselves.

**Challenge:** developmental capacity and recovery show no systematic change, or early compression frequency alone predicts later topology better than persistence and relational contingencies.

### Prediction 16: DIH alternatives diverge under stress

**[X]** Following corrective learning, a functional-overlay trajectory may resemble structural rewriting under low load but reactivate the older attractor more readily under high load. Genuine rewriting should produce durable changes in dependency edges and retain integration across a wider load range.

**Challenge:** proposed topology and stress-reactivation measures cannot distinguish rewriting, overlay, and partial irreversibility, or the distinctions do not predict longitudinal behavior.

## 13. Relationship to established theories

The model is a synthesis. Similarity to an established field does not validate the proposed mechanism, and apparent novelty should not be claimed without a systematic literature review.

| Established area | What it already contributes | Human Kernel reinterpretation or extension |
|---|---|---|
| Object relations and splitting | Good/bad self and object representations; difficulty integrating loved and hated aspects; idealization, devaluation, reparation | Models these as representational resolution, co-activation, topology, and competing stabilization routes |
| Attachment and co-regulation | Dependence on caregivers, security, rupture and repair, internal working models | Treats caregiver responses as a dynamic distribution that shapes strategy costs and external regulation dependence |
| Internalizing/externalizing research | Well-known distinction between inwardly and outwardly directed symptoms and behavior | Proposes that some inward and outward routes may be opposite solutions to one integration problem |
| Self-blame and attribution research | Links between global self-blame, shame, adversity, and internalizing outcomes; hostile or external attributions in externalizing outcomes | Focuses on where a relational system permits global badness and responsibility to be routed |
| Social learning and reinforcement | Behavior is shaped by consequences, modeling, and contingencies | Makes immediate regulation and attachment restoration the reinforcers of representational strategies |
| Family systems | Reciprocal dynamics, roles, scapegoating, triangles, and system-level patterns | Represents blame-carrying capacity and response distributions across a network rather than a single dyad |
| Mentalization and reflective functioning | Ability to represent minds, intentions, uncertainty, and multiple perspectives | Supplies possible buffer/integration nodes between external events and identity-level conclusions |
| Predictive processing and model updating | Priors, prediction error, precision, generative models, and belief updating | Uses the digital-twin analogy to contrast model updating with defensive preservation |
| Developmental psychopathology | Equifinality, multifinality, transactions, risk/protection, and trajectories | Frames outcomes as parameter-dependent attractors rather than single-cause disorders |
| Temperament, behavior genetics, and differential susceptibility | Heritable variation, biological sensitivity, and person–environment interaction | Treats inherited traits as parameters affecting costs and learning, not a prebuilt topology |
| Personality and narcissism theories | Grandiosity/vulnerability, self-esteem regulation, entitlement, shame, interpersonal dysfunction, defensive operations | Connects some narcissistic regulation to the other-compression route while explicitly rejecting diagnostic equivalence |
| Schema, cognitive, and learning models | Core beliefs, attributional styles, modes, avoidance, compensatory strategies | Recasts schemas as nodes and state-linked networks with updateability, centrality, and resolution |

### 13.1 Important distinction from Klein-derived ideas

The proposition that good and bad aspects of the same caregiver must become integrated has clear precedent in object relations theory. The Human Kernel model does **not** own that idea. Its proposed contribution is the mechanistic synthesis:

```text
one contradiction
    + parameterized integration costs
    + caregiver response distribution
    + blame/responsibility routing
    + reinforcement
    → alternative representational attractors
```

### 13.2 Important distinction from internalizing/externalizing

Saying “one child internalizes and another externalizes” is descriptive. The attractor-competition hypothesis becomes theoretically distinct only if it explains:

- why both can arise from a shared representational problem;
- what variables alter the relative cost of each route;
- how relational feedback reinforces the route;
- which new longitudinal or experimental predictions follow;
- why integration, as the alternative to low-resolution attractor competition, differs mechanistically from both compression routes.

If it fails to add these predictions, it should be regarded as a reframing rather than a new theory.

## 14. What is inherited versus potentially novel

### 14.1 Inherited or strongly precedent-based components

**[E/S]** None of the following should be claimed as novel:

- splitting or good/bad representations;
- partial versus whole representations of self and others;
- internalizing and externalizing trajectories;
- self-blame, shame, guilt, attribution, and defensive externalization;
- attachment, co-regulation, rupture, and repair;
- parenting contingencies and reinforcement;
- family roles and systemic blame patterns;
- genetic and temperamental contributions to personality;
- grandiose and vulnerable narcissistic regulation;
- mentalization and reflective buffering;
- internal generative models, prediction error, and model updating;
- developmental pathways, multifinality, and equifinality.

### 14.2 Potentially original synthesis

The following are candidate contributions, subject to literature review and empirical test:

1. **[H] Common-problem attractor competition:** self-compression and other-compression as alternative topology-preserving solutions to the same unresolved self–other contradiction.
2. **[H] Compression-cost principle:** after simultaneity fails, the system preferentially degrades whichever representation produces the lower combined attachment, self-continuity, affective, social, and Paper 10 structural-consequence cost.
3. **[H] Blame-carrier availability:** route stability depends partly on whether the relational network contains agents who can be made to carry global blame, or instead forces blame back into the child.
4. **[H] Response-distribution principle:** repeated probabilistic patterns across a network matter more than a single parenting category.
5. **[H/O] Differentiated responsibility as integration training:** high containment plus boundaries plus proportional responsibility teaches that no whole person must become the bad object.
6. **[H/X] Topological attractor account:** repeated local regulation updates produce self–other network structures with path dependence, world switching, and hysteresis.
7. **[X/O] Emergent Human Kernel simulation:** the two compression attractors and integrated trajectories arise from shared local rules without being explicitly encoded as outcomes.
8. **[H/O] Integration operator:** integration is specified as an effective-simultaneity gate followed by co-activation, state/source differentiation, error localization, responsibility estimation, response selection, outcome testing, and model update.
9. **[X/O] DIH discrimination:** structural rewriting, functional overlay, and partial irreversibility are separated as competing developmental-change models.

### 14.3 Novelty claim discipline

At present the strongest justified description is:

> **The Human Kernel developmental attractor competition model is a new or developing SRF application and mechanistic synthesis hypothesis, not yet a proven unique psychological theory.**

Determining novelty requires systematic searches across developmental psychopathology, object relations, attachment, family systems, social learning, attribution, personality, computational psychiatry, and dynamical-systems literature. Conversational searching or conceptual resemblance is insufficient for a priority claim.

## 16. Open questions

### 16.1 Mechanism

- What exactly constitutes the unresolved contradiction: caregiver ambivalence, self-aggression, attachment threat, shame, lack of mentalization, or some combination?
- Is compression best modeled as loss of simultaneity, valence collapse, causal attribution, precision weighting, graph disconnection, strategy selection, or state transition?
- Does effective simultaneity capacity precede route learning, or does relational learning build the capacity itself?
- Is the key bottleneck affect tolerance, working memory, representation complexity, self–other differentiation, or regulatory recovery?
- Are self-compression and other-compression truly symmetric at a deeper level, or only superficially parallel?

### 16.2 Timing and development

- Is there a sensitive period, or can equivalent attractors emerge at many ages?
- Does attractor consolidation occur gradually, through phase transitions, or through episodic reorganizations?
- Which developmental competencies are prerequisites for differentiated responsibility?
- How do language, autobiographical memory, theory of mind, and peer relationships change the dynamics?

### 16.3 Caregiver and network distribution

- How many interactions and how much consistency are required to stabilize a route?
- Can one reliable adult with high ACC and RRC offset a wider blaming network?
- How does caregiver unpredictability differ from consistently punitive or consistently capitulating responses?
- How do siblings, institutional settings, and culture allocate blame and status?
- Does the child learn from observed caregiver interactions even when not directly involved?

### 16.4 Temperament and genetics

- Which heritable traits alter the relative costs of self- versus other-compression?
- How large are evocative effects, where child behavior shapes caregiver response?
- Can genetic and environmental data distinguish predisposition from reinforced topology?
- Why do siblings diverge in the same nominal household?

### 16.5 Measurement

- How can simultaneous mixed representations be measured without relying only on self-report?
- Can blame routing be coded reliably from naturalistic interaction?
- How should ACC, UBA, and RRC be operationalized and validated?
- What measures distinguish accurate blame of genuine wrongdoing from defensive global other-blame?
- Can graph centrality, resolution, or world switching be inferred from narratives, behavior, experience sampling, or neural data?
- What would count as a real attractor rather than ordinary categorical clustering, and what additional result would justify the stronger term *bifurcation*?

### 16.6 Clinical and corrective change

- What experiences increase responsibility tolerance without triggering global shame?
- Does treatment need different entry points for self- and other-compression patterns?
- Can a relational system change one member’s attractor while continuing to reward the old role?
- How much repetition is needed to reverse hysteresis?
- Is repair the central mechanism, or one of several routes to greater updateability?

### 16.7 Boundary conditions

- When is global self-blame factually accurate, and when is strong other-blame a realistic response to abuse or exploitation?
- How does the model handle situations with extreme power asymmetry?
- Does it generalize beyond attachment-relevant conflict?
- Which disorders or ordinary personality differences require entirely different mechanisms?
- When does two-attractor competition plus the integration alternative oversimplify a multidimensional process?

### 16.8 Developmental irreversibility

- Can later experience change the original dependency topology, or mainly add compensatory routes around it?
- Which observations would distinguish structural rewriting from functional overlay?
- Does high load reveal an older attractor after apparently successful change?
- Are there age-sensitive limits on edge deletion, edge reversal, centrality change, or new-node formation?
- Is DIH global, domain-specific, node-specific, or false?
- Can a model infer partial irreversibility without building it into the learning rules?

## 17. Explicit caveats and prohibited inferences

### 17.1 This is a hypothesis, not an established fact

The unified developmental attractor-competition mechanism has not been validated. The document combines established concepts, SRF axioms, adjacent evidence, metaphor, and new formal proposals. It should generate research questions, not close them.

### 17.2 It is not a diagnostic model

The model cannot diagnose narcissistic personality disorder, trauma, attachment style, parenting quality, or any other clinical condition. No individual should be classified from a few anecdotes or conflict behaviors.

### 17.3 Do not equate other-compression with narcissism

Other-blame, anger, boundaries, protest, and recognition of another’s wrongdoing can be accurate and healthy. The hypothesized route concerns **recurrent global devaluation used to regulate threatened self-coherence**, not the mere act of blaming someone.

### 17.4 Do not equate self-compression with virtue or empathy

Chronic self-blame can be inaccurate and harmful. It does not prove superior empathy, morality, or insight. It may be a strategy for preserving attachment or control.

### 17.5 Do not blame caregivers simplistically

Caregivers have their own histories, temperaments, constraints, stressors, and networks. Child effects are reciprocal. Household averages can hide different child-specific interactions. A systemic mechanism is not a moral verdict.

### 17.6 Do not make genetic determinist claims

Heritability is population- and context-dependent and does not specify an individual destiny. Genetic influence on traits does not prove inheritance of a completed defensive structure.

### 17.7 Do not infer childhood causes from adult behavior alone

Retrospective narratives and current conflict patterns cannot uniquely identify a developmental path. Equifinality means multiple paths can produce similar outcomes; multifinality means similar experiences can produce different outcomes.

### 17.8 Do not erase real harm or power asymmetry

Integration does not require treating both parties as equally responsible. A victim’s recognition that another person acted abusively is not pathological splitting. Accurate protection may require distance, anger, legal action, or ending a relationship.

### 17.9 Do not literalize the computer metaphors

“Kernel,” “runtime,” “digital twin,” “node,” “topology,” “compression,” and “process isolation” are explanatory and formal modeling tools. They are not established anatomical modules or direct descriptions of neural implementation.

### 17.10 Do not confuse simulation with validation

A simulation shows consequences of its assumptions. It becomes scientifically informative only through transparent assumptions, alternative models, sensitivity analysis, empirical contact, and the genuine possibility of failure.

### 17.11 Avoid categorical and stigmatizing language

Prefer:

- “a person using an externalizing or other-compression strategy”;
- “narcissistic traits or defenses”;
- “a hypothesized self–other topology.”

Avoid treating “narcissist” as a complete explanation of a person.

### 17.12 Not medical advice

This model is not a substitute for assessment, treatment, safeguarding, or emergency support by qualified professionals.

## 18. Glossary

**Affective Containment Capacity (ACC) [O]**  
Capacity to tolerate another person’s intense affect or accusation without retaliation, collapse, abandonment, or immediate surrender of boundaries.

**Attractor [H/X]**  
A recurrent region of state space toward which the system tends to return. Here it refers to a learned, self-reinforcing regulatory and representational pattern.

**Attractor competition [H/O]**  
A dynamic in which multiple stable or metastable regulatory configurations compete, with selection probability and basins of attraction depending on state, structure, environment, and history. This is the preferred description of the current model.

**Bifurcation [reserved mathematical term]**  
A qualitative change in system behavior or attractor structure when a defined control parameter crosses a critical value. The current model has not demonstrated this. *Bifurcation model* is retained only as the former working name.

**Blame routing [H]**  
The recurrent placement of global badness or fault in the self, the other, a scapegoat, or no stable carrier.

**Caregiver response distribution [H/O]**  
The probability distribution of responses the child encounters across people, contexts, and time, rather than a single fixed “parenting style.”

**Contradiction load [O]**  
The current affective and representational demand created by mutually difficult information about self, other, and attachment.

**Digital twin [M]**  
An internal-model analogy emphasizing prediction, simulation, feedback, and continuous updating.

**Differentiated responsibility [H/O]**  
Allocation of responsibility to specific actions and agents in proportion to their causal and moral role, without making an entire person globally bad.

**Developmental Irreversibility Hypothesis (DIH) [OPEN]**  
The unresolved SRF hypothesis that some structures formed or missed during development may not be fully rewritable later. This model distinguishes true structural rewriting, functional overlay, and partial irreversibility rather than assuming one of them.

**Effective simultaneity capacity [H/O]**  
The state-dependent capacity to keep differentiated conflicting representations active together after developmental capacity, caregiver scaffolding, learned regulation, arousal, fatigue, and current load are considered.

**Human Kernel [M/H]**  
A systems framing of psychological development around continuity, regulation, representation, self–other separation, resource limits, and fault tolerance.

**Integration**  
Following simultaneity, the operation that represents the relationship between conflicting differentiated contents without deleting either one. It includes source differentiation, error localization, responsibility estimation, response selection, outcome testing, and model update.

**Other-compression [H]**  
A hypothesized low-resolution route that preserves the self’s global goodness or coherence by degrading the other-representation into global badness. *Other-sacrifice* is its readable but non-technical alias.

**Process isolation [M/H]**  
Capacity for one person’s state to affect another without directly becoming or globally rewriting the other’s state.

**Representational resolution [H/O]**  
The amount of relevant complexity and contextual variation an internal model preserves and can use.

**Responsibility Redistribution Capacity (RRC) [O]**  
Capacity to accept one’s actual share, reject false global blame, return another’s share non-humiliatingly, maintain boundaries, and support repair.

**Self-compression [H]**  
A hypothesized low-resolution route that preserves the caregiver, attachment, or relationship as globally good/necessary by degrading the self-representation into global badness. *Self-sacrifice* is its readable but non-technical alias.

**Simultaneity Principle (SRF Paper 9)**  
The principle that differentiated representations must remain active together before they can be integrated. Simultaneity is a mechanism and prerequisite, not the same thing as wholeness.

**Structural Consequences Axiom (SRF Paper 10)**  
The proposition that pressure to protect or preserve a representation depends on the downstream structural consequences of its loss or degradation, not only on its content or emotional intensity.

**Undifferentiated Blame Assumption (UBA) [O]**  
Tendency to accept inaccurate, unlimited, or global blame and surrender boundaries in order to restore regulation or connection.

**Updateability [H/O]**  
The degree to which a representation can change in proportion to new evidence without being either rigidly protected or chaotically overwritten.

**World-model switch [H/X]**  
A broad change in linked self, other, future, and safety representations triggered by a central state or valence shift.

## 19. Guidance for downstream AI models

When using this document as context:

1. Treat the Human Kernel developmental attractor competition model as a **working SRF developmental application and theoretical hypothesis**.
2. Preserve the epistemic labels and distinguish Paper 9, Paper 10, established precedent, and the model’s novel developmental extension.
3. Do not diagnose individuals or infer childhood history from adult anecdotes.
4. Do not equate other-compression with NPD or self-compression with empathy.
5. Keep integration outside low-resolution attractor competition and make it available only through a defined effective-simultaneity gate.
6. Model route selection as probabilistic, reciprocal, developmental, and usually repeated—not conscious or instantaneous.
7. Treat genetics as parameters affecting sensitivity and costs, not a predetermined topology.
8. Treat caregiver behavior as a distribution across a network and over time, not a single mother-causes-child story.
9. Distinguish affect containment from accepting false blame; preserve the ACC/UBA/RRC decomposition.
10. Use *self-compression* and *other-compression* technically; treat *self-sacrifice* and *other-sacrifice* only as non-intentional readable aliases.
11. Reserve *bifurcation* for a demonstrated parameter-driven qualitative transition; call the present model *attractor competition*.
12. If proposing a simulation, allow all outcomes and null results to emerge. Do not hard-code the theory’s conclusion or DIH.
13. Distinguish structural rewriting, functional overlay, and stress reactivation when discussing later change.
14. If discussing originality, call the model a **candidate SRF developmental extension and mechanistic synthesis** pending systematic literature review and empirical test.
15. When generating new claims, label them `[H]`, `[X]`, or `[O]` unless they are independently supported by cited research.

### Compact context block

> The Human Kernel developmental attractor competition model is an SRF developmental application. Paper 9 supplies its processing constraint: differentiated conflicting representations must remain simultaneously active for integration to occur. Paper 10 supplies its topological constraint: the cost of degrading a representation depends on the downstream structure that relies on it. When effective simultaneity capacity is insufficient for a loved caregiver’s goodness and frustration or harmfulness to remain active together, two low-resolution configurations may compete. In self-compression (“I am bad”), self-representational complexity is lost while the caregiver and attachment remain globally good. In other-compression (“You are bad”), the other’s complexity is lost while self-coherence remains globally good. The readable aliases self-sacrifice and other-sacrifice do not imply intention. Integration is not a third compression attractor: when the simultaneity gate is open, it co-activates the representations, differentiates state ownership, localizes error, estimates responsibility, selects a response, tests the outcome, and updates the model without deleting either person. Its reinforcement comes from preserved connection, successful repair, improved prediction, effective agency, and distress reduction without global devaluation. Which compression attractor stabilizes is hypothesized to depend on structural-consequence costs, temperament and partly heritable parameters, developmental state, caregiver/network response distributions, blame routing, immediate regulation, and reinforcement history. The preferred technical name is attractor competition, because no true mathematical bifurcation has been demonstrated. DIH remains open: later change may reflect structural rewriting, functional overlay, or partial irreversibility. All model-specific mechanisms remain unvalidated hypotheses rather than established facts.

## 20. Research program in priority order

1. **Conceptual clarification:** define self-compression, other-compression, integration, contradiction load, effective simultaneity, structural cost, and blame routing so they do not merely rename existing constructs.
2. **SRF dependency specification:** formalize exactly which Paper 9 and Paper 10 propositions are imported and which developmental claims are new.
3. **Systematic literature review:** assess overlap and novelty across the relevant fields.
4. **Measurement development:** operationalize ACC, UBA, RRC, simultaneity margin, integration steps, state switching, responsibility accuracy, and topology change.
5. **Developmental observational coding:** study real caregiver–child interaction sequences, immediate regulation outcomes, and age-related change.
6. **Longitudinal analysis:** test whether contingencies predict within-child change beyond baseline traits and family averages.
7. **Computational implementation:** test attractor emergence, parameter sensitivity, the integration gate, structural consequence costs, and alternative mechanisms.
8. **DIH model comparison:** distinguish structural rewriting, functional overlay, partial irreversibility, and stress reactivation.
9. **Bifurcation test only if justified:** identify a control parameter and test for a qualitative stability change before restoring the stronger term.
10. **Pre-registered falsification tests:** specify which results would reject or substantially revise the model.
11. **Clinical translation only after validation:** avoid diagnostic or treatment claims until mechanisms and measures show reliable empirical value.

The model’s scientific value will not be determined by how intuitively compelling its story feels. It will be determined by whether it can be defined precisely, distinguished from existing explanations, used to make risky predictions, and revised or rejected in response to evidence.
